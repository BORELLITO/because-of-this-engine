On January 24, 1961, a B-52 broke apart over North Carolina — and two hydrogen bombs fell with it.

One fell free into a muddy field.

The other opened its parachute and came down nearly intact.

Inside, the crash had already started the arming sequence.

Wires pulled. The thermal battery activated. The weapon moved through the steps designed to make it explode.

But one final setting was still on SAFE.

It hit the ground without a nuclear detonation.

Three crew members died. The weapon was buried, recovered, and taken apart.

Investigators found a safety system that could move dangerously close to detonation during an accident.

The terrifying part was not simply that a bomb had fallen.

It was that the safety design had almost completed its job.

North Carolina was spared by the last switch that had not moved.

Not a command. Not a miracle. A setting — and a machine that came far too close to doing exactly what it was built to do.
