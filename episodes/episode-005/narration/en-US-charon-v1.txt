Just after midnight on September 26, 1983, a Soviet warning system reported five American nuclear missiles.

Inside a bunker outside Moscow, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov had minutes to decide what happened next.

His job was to assess the alert and pass it up the chain. If he called it real, Soviet command could move toward retaliation.

Then came another launch. Then another. Five in total.

But Petrov saw what the machine could not: it made no sense. A first strike would not begin with only five missiles. Ground radar showed nothing.

He reported it false.

The bunker waited.

No missiles arrived.

The cause was almost absurd: sunlight reflecting off high clouds had fooled a satellite.

Petrov was not celebrated. The failure embarrassed the system, and he was reprimanded over a logging error.

History later called him the man who saved the world. The truer version is quieter:

He refused to turn uncertainty into catastrophe.
