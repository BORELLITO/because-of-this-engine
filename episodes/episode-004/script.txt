What if the tallest tsunami ever recorded began with a mountain falling into a quiet bay? On July ninth, nineteen fifty-eight, a magnitude seven point eight earthquake shook southeast Alaska. In the bay, the shaking ripped the rock from the mountainside. It plunged into the narrow inlet like an impact. Water shot up the opposite slope to one thousand seven hundred and twenty feet, higher than the Empire State Building. The wave stripped forests, erased cabins, and carried boats across the land. One fisherman watched as the surge lifted his boat above the trees. Two people died; others survived because the bay was nearly empty. The bay is narrow and steep, like a giant bathtub. The landslide displaced water, while the walls trapped its energy and sent it racing back and forth. Scientists later found the scars still visible in the forest. Because of this, researchers began treating landslides as tsunami threats. A tsunami does not need an ocean. Sometimes, all it needs is a mountainside, a narrow bay, and a few seconds of silence.
