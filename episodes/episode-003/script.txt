What if a lake could kill without making a sound?

On August twenty-first, nineteen eighty-six, families living near Lake Nyos in Cameroon went to sleep beside water that looked completely calm.

During the night, the lake released a massive cloud of carbon dioxide.

The gas was invisible, odorless, and heavier than air. It rolled downhill into nearby villages, replacing the oxygen people were breathing.

By morning, more than seventeen hundred people and thousands of animals were dead.

There was no fire. No battlefield. No visible warning.

Scientists later discovered that volcanic activity beneath the lake had been feeding carbon dioxide into its deep water for years.

A rare limnic eruption suddenly released that trapped gas.

The exact trigger is still debated, but the result was undeniable: a silent cloud moving through the darkness.

Engineers eventually installed pipes to drain gas from the lake’s depths and reduce the danger.

But Lake Nyos left behind a terrifying lesson.

Some of the deadliest disasters do not look dangerous until they have already begun.

Because of this, other volcanic lakes are now monitored for the same hidden threat.
