What if a lake could kill without making a sound?

On August twenty-first, nineteen eighty-six, families living near Lake Nios went to sleep beside still calm water.

During the night, the lake released a massive cloud of carbon dioxide.

The gas was invisible, odorless, and heavier than air.

It rolled downhill into nearby villages, replacing the oxygen people were breathing.

By morning, more than seventeen hundred people and animals were dead.

There was no fire. No battlefield. No visible warning.

Scientists discovered volcanic activity beneath the lake had fed carbon dioxide into deep water for years.

A rare limnic eruption released that trapped gas. The exact trigger is still debated, but a silent cloud moved through the darkness.

Engineers installed pipes to drain gas from the lake’s depths and reduce thuh danger.

But Lake Nios left behind a terrifying lesson about hidden danger.

Some of the deadliest disasters do not look dangerous until they begin.

Because of this, other volcanic lakes are now monitored for the same threat.
