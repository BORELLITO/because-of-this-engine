Howard Ulrich was asleep in a small boat in Alaska's Lituya Bay when the mountain above his seven-year-old son began to move. On July ninth, nineteen fifty-eight, a magnitude seven point nine earthquake tore the shoreline open. Howard woke to the sound of rock and ice collapsing into Gilbert Inlet. The impact did not make an ordinary wave. It threw water up the opposite slope, one thousand seven hundred and twenty feet—higher than the Empire State Building. The surge stripped the forest and carried debris across the land. Howard could not free the anchor. He paid out the chain, started the engine, and waited. The Edrie rose with the wall of water and was carried toward shore. Then the backwash pulled the boat away. Another boat sank near the entrance. Two men died. Howard and his son survived, but the forest kept the evidence: a clean scar thousands of feet above the tide. Lituya Bay proved a tsunami does not need an ocean. Sometimes a mountain is enough.
