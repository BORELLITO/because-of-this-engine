At 2:17 a.m., Daniel was alone in the hospital switchboard when an impossible extension rang. The number belonged to a ward disconnected eleven years earlier—the night his sister died. He answered. No voice spoke, only breathing, and three words repeated: Don't send the elevator. The call ended, but the switchboard printed tomorrow's emergency log, already signed with Daniel's name. He checked the fourth floor. The hospital had thirteen floors, yet one elevator button was warm. Beneath it, a red light pulsed behind a sealed records door. Daniel had spent eleven years filing his sister's death as an accident. Now the missing call was replaying the exact minute he had refused to remember. He found the original log—and saw the rule: every answered call demanded a witness. If he destroyed the page, the past would stay buried. If he routed it, someone tomorrow might live. Daniel connected the line. The hospital went dark, and the receiver began ringing from inside the sealed room. At dawn, the new log contained one completed call: Daniel's name—followed by the word home.
